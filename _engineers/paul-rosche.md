## What he did

Paul Rosche is the BMW engine man who led the project for the **1.5-litre turbocharged four-cylinder** with which Nelson Piquet and [Gordon Murray]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/gordon-murray.md %})'s Brabham BT52 won the 1983 world championship: the first drivers' title won with a turbo engine{% include src.html n="1,2,3" %}.

## Before F1

- Born in Munich in 1934, he joined BMW in 1957, straight after his studies, and stayed until 1999 (BMW){% include src.html n="1" %}.
- Camshaft calculations were his favourite area, hence his nickname "Nocken-Paule" (BMW){% include src.html n="1" %}.
- In 1969, he designed the 2-litre turbo engine with which BMW won the European Touring Car Championship (BMW){% include src.html n="1,2" %}.
- In 1975, he joined BMW Motorsport GmbH to head the design of the BMW M1 engines; he was technical director of BMW Motorsport from 1979 to 1996 (BMW, 2014){% include src.html n="2" %}.

## The Brabham turbo engine

- In 1980, with Dieter Stappert, he laid the foundation for BMW's first F1 involvement and headed the engine project (BMW, 2016){% include src.html n="1" %}. According to Autosport, the project had been started in the 1970s against the will of company management{% include src.html n="6" %}.
- His team turned a 1.5-litre production four-cylinder into a championship engine: sixteen valves, a [turbocharger]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#turbocompresseur) and, a first in F1 according to BMW, digital engine electronics{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. BMW gives an initial output of around 800hp{% include src.html n="1,2" %}.
- 1981, the development year of the M12/13, and 1982, its first season, were difficult: the engine was fragile (Motor Sport, 2001){% include src.html n="3" %}. Murray recalls that Rosche was adamant about a single turbo, initially a KKK truck unit{% include src.html n="3" %}.
- Rosche promised big power gains once reliability was sorted; according to Murray, that promise and the skirt ban shaped the design of the BT52 (Motor Sport, 2001){% include src.html n="3" %}.
- Tests showed detonation beyond 650bhp; Rosche called on BASF for a purpose-made fuel, used in 1983 (Autosport){% include src.html n="6" %}.
- In 1983, when Renault froze the specification of its engine, Rosche pressed on with development, squeezing ever more power from the four-cylinder (Motor Sport, 2009){% include src.html n="4" %}.
- The exact output remains unknown: according to Rosche, "around 1400hp", as the dyno did not go beyond 1280hp (BMW){% include src.html n="1,2" %}. Motor Sport gives over 1400bhp in qualifying trim in 1983{% include src.html n="5" %}.
- After the title, rumours of illegal fuel circulated; the FISA report found it compliant, and Rosche always insisted there had been no cheating (Motor Sport, 2009){% include src.html n="4" %}.
- According to BMW, when BMW withdrew from F1 in 1987, the engine had nine Grand Prix wins to its name{% include src.html n="1,2" %}.

## The return to F1

- After 1987, he remained technical director of BMW M GmbH until 1996, then, as technical director and managing director of BMW Motorsport Limited, he led BMW back into F1 as an engine manufacturer (BMW, 2016){% include src.html n="1" %}.
- Motor Sport calls him the inspiration for this second involvement: when Renault announced its departure at the end of 1997, Rosche persuaded BMW's management to partner with Williams{% include src.html n="4" %}. The debut was set for 2000; Rosche retired in the meantime, replaced by Gerhard Berger and Mario Theissen{% include src.html n="4" %}.
- The sources consulted make him the leader of this return; none credits him with designing the BMW [V10]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#architecture-moteur).

## Other engines

According to BMW, his department also produced the 2-litre four-valve Formula 2 four-cylinder (more than 150 wins, six European titles) and the 6-litre V12 that won Le Mans in 1995 and 1999; the engine of the first BMW M3 was developed under his leadership{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. He died in Munich in November 2016, aged 82{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Attribution

The engine was a BMW team effort, and its success also owed much to fuel and electronics: BMW speaks of "Rosche and his team"{% include src.html n="1,2,6" %}. Sources differ on its race debut: Motor Sport (2009) writes that it made its debut at the first race of 1981, but later quotes Dieter Stappert, according to whom the debut was postponed to the first Grand Prix of 1982{% include src.html n="4" %}; BMW also gives the start of the 1982 season{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. This site follows 1982.
