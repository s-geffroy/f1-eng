## What he did

Gianni Lancia was not a design-office engineer: he was the **head of Lancia**, son of the founder Vincenzo Lancia, running the family company with his mother Adele{% include src.html n="1" %}. He decided to take Lancia into F1 and commissioned the **Lancia D50**, designed by a team led by [Vittorio Jano]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/vittorio-jano.md %}){% include src.html n="1,2" %}.

## The commissioner

- After 1952, he decided the works team would drop the Aurelia coupés for a true series of sports racing cars; according to Doug Nye, Lancia then won the Carrera Panamericana, the Targa Florio and the Mille Miglia in 1953-1954{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- The next step in his ambitions was the new 2.5-litre F1: the experimental department started the programme in August 1953{% include src.html n="1" %}. He set a debut at the French Grand Prix of 4 July 1954 as the target; the D50 only raced late in the season, at the Spanish Grand Prix in Barcelona{% include src.html n="1,2" %}.
- According to Chris Nixon, he persuaded Alberto Ascari to leave Scuderia Ferrari by outlining his plans and revealing the details of the new car; Ascari brought Gigi Villoresi with him{% include src.html n="2" %}.

## His part in the design

The two Motor Sport articles do not say the same thing, and this site does not decide between them.

- **Doug Nye** credits him with no design work: the car's principles were Jano's, the engine was detailed with [Ettore Zaccone Mina]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/ettore-zaccone-mina.md %}), and Nye credits Jano with the idea of the pannier tanks{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **Chris Nixon**, reporting Zaccone Mina's account, gives him a more direct role{% include src.html n="2" %}. The [stressed engine]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#moteur-porteur) and the placing of the fuel tanks came out of the team's discussions, "with Gianni Lancia making the final decisions"{% include src.html n="2" %}. Several innovations are said to have come from his "fertile brain", and the elegant **pannier fuel tanks**, between the wheels, are presented as his idea{% include src.html n="2" %}.

The accurate wording: Gianni Lancia was the commissioner and final arbiter of the project, credited by Nixon with the idea of the pannier tanks, which Nye instead attributes to Jano{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. He was not its designer, even though Nixon presents the D50 as his brainchild and credits him with several of its innovations{% include src.html n="2" %}.

## The end

- According to Nye, for five years he let his engineers' creativity run riot, almost regardless of cost{% include src.html n="1" %}. The company was in deep financial trouble, and the family sold it to the financier Carlo Pesenti{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- After Ascari's death at Monza in 1955, Lancia announced its withdrawal from racing{% include src.html n="1,2" %}.
- On 26 July 1955, six of the eight D50s were handed over to Ferrari in Turin{% include src.html n="1" %}. The two accounts differ on this transfer: Nixon writes that the Italian Automobile Club decided to give the cars to Ferrari, with Fiat contributing 50 million lire over five years{% include src.html n="2" %}; Nye mentions annual sponsorship of some £30,000 given to Ferrari by Agnelli of Fiat, Gianni Lancia's "friend"{% include src.html n="1" %}.
