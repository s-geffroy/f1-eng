## The question transformed

**With what materials and interfaces should the modern F1 car be built?**

## 1981 — McLaren MP4/1: carbon

The MP4/1 was the first F1 car built around a **full [carbon composite]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#composite-carbone) [monocoque]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#monocoque)** to race in a Grand Prix; the Lotus 88, which appeared before it with a carbon chassis, was never allowed to race.

Carbon was born of an aerodynamic constraint: to widen the wings under the car, Barnard wanted a chassis much narrower at the bottom, which costs a great deal of torsional stiffness. He needed a new material; he found it after a visit to British Aerospace, which was making composite cowlings. [Steve Nichols]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/steve-nichols.md %}) steered him towards Hercules Aerospace, which moulded a raw tub; McLaren trimmed it and bonded it together from five pieces. Barnard designed the car surrounded notably by Steve Nichols and [Alan Jenkins]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/alan-jenkins.md %}).

The effects touched stiffness, weight, safety, architecture and the industrialisation of composites all at once. The MP4/1 won the 1981 British Grand Prix, the first victory for a carbon composite car; John Watson's crash at Monza the same year put an end, according to Barnard, to the myths about carbon's fragility. The principle became universal.

## 1984 — McLaren MP4/2: the engine to his dimensions

For the MP4/2, Barnard imposed on Porsche the dimensions of the TAG turbo V6, to serve the aerodynamics of the underbody. The car won 12 of the 16 races of 1984.

## 1989 — Ferrari 640: the semi-automatic gearbox

Based in a design office in England, the Guildford Technical Office, Barnard introduced on the Ferrari 640 the **[semi-automatic gearbox]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#boite-semi-automatique) controlled from the steering wheel**, originally planned for the 639, which never raced:

- gradual disappearance of the H-pattern lever;
- fewer gear-change errors;
- hands kept on the wheel;
- a more compact cockpit;
- an opening towards electronic management of the transmission.

The same car introduced front suspension with short torsion bars, a fashion that lasted. The 640 won first time out in Brazil, then in Hungary and Portugal.

## Technical career

- A graduate of Watford College of Technology, he joined Lola, where he drew the T250 for Formula Super Vee.
- At McLaren from 1972, he worked with Gordon Coppuck on the M16 IndyCar and on the world-championship-winning M23.
- At Vel's Parnelli Jones, he designed the VPJ-6, the first Cosworth DFX-engined IndyCar. He then drew the Chaparral 2K, the first full [ground effect]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#effet-de-sol) IndyCar, with which Johnny Rutherford won the 1980 Indianapolis 500.
- Back at Ferrari in the 1990s, he drew the F310, with which Michael Schumacher won three races in 1996. He then bought Ferrari's English design office, which became B3 Technologies.
- The Arrows A19 is the last F1 car fully credited to Barnard.

## Attribution

The idea of the paddles itself did not come from him: Barnard says it was suggested by someone in his design office, on the model of karts; Motorsport.com attributes it to Piero Lardi Ferrari. Barnard remains the one who designed the system and introduced it in racing.

## Why number two

Two **independent** breakthroughs, both of which became standards, and clearly documented direct authorship: he is probably the revolutionary with the clearest personal authorship in the whole history of F1.

He stays behind [Colin Chapman]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/colin-chapman.md %}) on historical breadth: fewer breakthroughs, less repetition. But the gap is narrow. If the weighting for "direct authorship" were raised significantly, Barnard could move ahead.

## Verdict

Chapman's main challenger, and the clearest of the revolutionaries with personal authorship.
