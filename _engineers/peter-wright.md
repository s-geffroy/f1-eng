## A historical correction

Peter Wright's high placing in this ranking corrects an attribution: that of [ground effect]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#effet-de-sol) to [Colin Chapman]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/colin-chapman.md %}) alone. formula1.com explicitly links Wright and [Tony Rudd]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/tony-rudd.md %}) to the research carried out at BRM at the end of the 1960s, then at Lotus, which led to the Lotus 78 and 79.

## The ground effect chain

1. **BRM.** Wright began his own "[wing car]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#voiture-aile)" there, an idea that would evolve into the Lotus 78. In 1969, Rudd, who was hostile to [wings]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#aileron), suggested that the bodywork itself should produce the [downforce]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#appui); Wright measured unprecedented downforce levels in the [wind tunnel]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#soufflerie) at Imperial College, but the project was stopped in June 1969.
2. **March.** At Specialised Mouldings, he designed the wing-section sidepods of the March 701.
3. **Lotus.** In August 1975, Chapman handed his wing car brief to Rudd. Wright ran the wind tunnel tests at Imperial College; the first Lotus 78 was running in private testing in the second half of 1976.
4. **Lotus 78, then 79.** The principle then spread to the whole grid.

## After ground effect

- He played a key role in the design of the twin-chassis Lotus 88.
- Lotus's **[active suspension]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#suspension-active)**, which he developed from Cranfield's work (David Williams), was first used on Nigel Mansell's Lotus 92, in the first two races of 1983; in 1987 Ayrton Senna's Lotus 99T became the first F1 car with active suspension to win a Grand Prix.
- In 1990, he took over Team Lotus with Peter Collins, and left the team when it closed in 1994.
- At the FIA, he contributed to the development of the HANS device and of cockpits able to withstand 60g.

## Attribution

The contribution remains collective. formula1.com credits the design of the Lotus 78 to [Ralph Bellamy]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/ralph-bellamy.md %}) and [Tony Southgate]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/tony-southgate.md %}); the other open sources divide the roles as follows: Bellamy drew the car and the chassis, [Martin Ogilvie]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/martin-ogilvie.md %}) the suspension, Wright the aerodynamics, and Southgate took part in the development.

## Why Tier B

Wright is a major causal player in two of the great revolutions in F1, ground effect and active suspension. The ground effect breakthrough belongs to a programme led by Chapman: that is what keeps him out of Tier A.
