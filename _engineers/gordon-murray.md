## What he changed

Murray combines conceptual radicalism with pragmatism{% include src.html n="3,4,9" %}. Promoted to chief designer at Brabham, he produced several landmark cars there{% include src.html n="6" %}.

- **The Brabham BT46B (1978)**, designed with the involvement of [David North]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/david-north.md %}){% include src.html n="14" %}, carried a large vertical [fan]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#voiture-a-ventilateur) under the rear [wing]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#aileron), officially to cool the Alfa Romeo [flat-12]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#flat-12){% include src.html n="7" %}. It won the Swedish Grand Prix with Niki Lauda and raced only once: sources differ on how it came to be withdrawn, as the [car's page]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _cars/brabham-bt46b.md %}) explains{% include src.html n="2,7" %}.
- **The modern tactical pit stop.** [Refuelling]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#ravitaillement) returned in 1982 thanks to Brabham and Murray{% include src.html n="8" %}. Riccardo Patrese took on four new tyres and 24 gallons of fuel in 15.6 seconds: the first routine modern stop{% include src.html n="8" %}.
- **The Brabham BT52 (1983).** After the announcement, on 3 November 1982, of the mandatory [flat bottom]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#fond-plat), which banned [ground effect]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#effet-de-sol) underbodies and [skirts]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#jupes){% include src.html n="1,9" %}, the BT52, every part of which Murray says he and David North drew between them{% include src.html n="1" %}, was designed and built in six weeks{% include src.html n="9" %}. Murray himself speaks of three months{% include src.html n="11" %}. With it, Nelson Piquet became the first world champion with a turbo engine, and Murray's modular system was then widely copied{% include src.html n="9" %}.
- **McLaren and the MP4/4.** Murray then joined McLaren, where the MP4/4 was born; it won 15 of the 16 races of 1988{% include src.html n="6" %}.

## Attribution: the MP4/4

formula1.com attributes the MP4/4 sometimes to Murray, in its vote on the most influential technical innovator{% include src.html n="3,4" %}, sometimes to Steve Nichols, in its write-up of the Beyond the Grid podcast{% include src.html n="5" %}. Its authorship is disputed{% include src.html n="10" %}. According to Autosport, Murray and [Steve Nichols]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/steve-nichols.md %}), then McLaren's technical director and chief designer respectively, have both been presented as its designer; Nichols considers Murray's role "more directorial than technical"{% include src.html n="10" %}. Autosport nevertheless concludes that the detail design was down to Nichols and his team, but that Murray, as befits a technical director, was at the heart of the concept{% include src.html n="10" %}. Cautious wording is called for: Murray played a central role in the MP4/4's concept; he is not its sole author. The design team disputes his account: according to Motor Sport, its seventeen members had listed 82 inaccuracies in writing in Ian Bamsey's book crediting the car to Murray{% include src.html n="12" %}, and [Matthew Jeffreys]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/matthew-jeffreys.md %}), project leader for the monocoque and front suspension, was still publicly disputing it in 2026{% include src.html n="13" %}.

## Why Tier B

His ideas are strong and often unexpected, but the **spread of some of his breakthroughs remains uneven**. The BT46B raced only once{% include src.html n="7" %}: a withdrawn innovation keeps its value as a breakthrough, without proving lasting influence. The tactical pit stop, which the other teams did not copy straight away (at Rio in 1983, only Williams also tried refuelling{% include src.html n="1" %}), and the BT52's modular system, widely copied{% include src.html n="9" %}, were on the other hand taken up{% include src.html n="1,8" %}.
