"Who won the most?" is not the right question. A record of results measures what a car achieved; it does not tell you whether that car forced the rest of the grid to change the way it designed an F1 car. This ranking looks for something else: **an engineer's capacity to shift the technical frontier of the sport for good.**

Four confusions must be avoided from the outset:

- **titles** are not **innovation**;
- **innovation** is not **invention**;
- **invention** is not **diffusion**;
- **technical direction** is not **personal authorship**.

## The weighting

Each engineer receives a score out of 100, built from five criteria:

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Identifiable technical breakthrough | **30%** |
| Lasting influence on F1 car design | **35%** |
| Breadth of the field transformed | **15%** |
| Sporting results directly linked to the innovations | **10%** |
| Personal authorship / traceability of the contribution | **10%** |

Lasting influence is the dominant criterion. Sporting results count for only 10%: this is deliberate.

> **These scores are not historical facts.** They are a comparative instrument. A gap of 1 or 2 points does not reflect a scientific difference in merit.

## The secondary diagnostics

Other questions are used to **correct and interpret** the score, without being mechanically added to it (that would count the same thing twice).

- **Nature of the breakthrough**: local innovation, subsystem innovation, architectural revolution, change in the design process, paradigm shift.
- **Diffusion**: limited to one team, copied by several rivals, standard across the grid, structural principle of F1.
- **Longevity**: a concept that survives several sets of regulations weighs more than a temporary innovation.
- **Banned innovation**: a ban does not erase the value of a breakthrough, but nor does it prove lasting influence.
- **Repetition**: staying at the frontier across several regulatory generations is a test of robustness.
- **Gap to the state of the art**: we distinguish between the lead over its own era and the absolute impact on later F1.
- **Counterfactual**: without this engineer, would F1 have adopted the same solution at the same time? An almost inevitable evolution, the acceleration of a mature evolution, a breakthrough unlikely in the short term, or the creation of a new trajectory?
- **Object or process**: changing the car and changing *the way the car is designed* are two different things.
- **Operational engineering**: telemetry, simulation, software and race methods count as soon as they lastingly transform the profession.

## The lone-genius trap

F1 history loves isolated heroes. Yet a single revolution involves several roles:

1. the **inventor** of the concept;
2. the **first to apply** it in F1;
3. the **technical architect** of the programme;
4. the **developer** who makes the principle effective;
5. the **industrialiser** who makes it reliable and reproducible;
6. the **technical leader** who organises the team;
7. the **diffuser** who turns it into a standard.

The rule is simple: **the same contribution is not credited in full to more than one person.** Four cases illustrate the difficulty.

**Lotus ground effect.** [Peter Wright]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/peter-wright.md %}) and [Tony Rudd]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/tony-rudd.md %}) had worked on the concept at BRM. At Lotus, [Colin Chapman]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/colin-chapman.md %}) asked them to rethink the fundamental configuration of an F1 car; the wind tunnel work led to the Lotus 78, with Peter Wright on aerodynamics. Sources differ on who drew it: according to Doug Nye (Motor Sport), [Ralph Bellamy]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/ralph-bellamy.md %}) set out its layout and chassis and [Martin Ogilvie]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/martin-ogilvie.md %}) detailed its suspension; according to formula1.com, it was designed by Bellamy and [Tony Southgate]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/tony-southgate.md %}), whom GrandPrix.com places on the development of the 78 alongside Peter Wright. Chapman initiated and directed the programme that made ground effect workable and dominant; he did not invent it.

**Cooper.** [John Cooper]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/john-cooper.md %}) established the rear-engine layout with a design team in which [Owen Maddock]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/owen-maddock.md %}) played a central role.

**Cosworth.** The DFV is a Cosworth achievement, with [Keith Duckworth]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/keith-duckworth.md %}) as the central technical figure and [Mike Costin]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/mike-costin.md %}) as co-founder and a major contributor.

**Williams active suspension.** The success of the active-suspension Williams cars came from a chain that included, among others, [Patrick Head]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/patrick-head.md %}) (overall architecture and direction), [Frank Dernie]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/frank-dernie.md %}) (designer of the first system, on the FW11B in 1987), then [Steve Wise]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/steve-wise.md %}) (the hardware) and [Paddy Lowe]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/paddy-lowe.md %}) (most of the software), who arrived in 1987, and [Adrian Newey]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/adrian-newey.md %}) (chief designer of the FW14B under Head's technical direction, then joint technical direction of the FW15C). Credit is shared out according to each person's actual role.

## What the method produces

It produces four tiers: five paradigm revolutionaries (Tier A), then fifteen engineers, spread across Tiers B, C and D, who transformed major subsystems, methods or technical trajectories.

## How to read an entry
{: #lire-une-fiche}

The panel of the twenty ranked engineers carries four indications.

- **Rank**: the position in the ranking, from 1 to 20.
- **Tier**: the level of impact, defined on the [tiers page]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/tiers/). The tier does not follow from a score threshold: it comes from a qualitative judgement based on the secondary diagnostics (nature of the breakthrough, diffusion, longevity). The score then orders the engineers, so the tiers form contiguous score bands: 91.5 to 96 for Tier A, 85 to 90 for Tier B, 79 to 83.5 for Tier C, 73.5 to 77.5 for Tier D.
- **Score**: the mark out of 100 produced by the five weighted criteria above. It is a comparative instrument, not a fact.
- **Confidence**: how solid the attribution is, that is, how certain we are about what belongs personally to the engineer, given the sources. It falls when accounts contradict each other, when the attribution is disputed or when sources are scarce. It ranges from "Medium" to "Very high".

Unranked entries have no rank, tier, score or confidence.

## Unranked entries
{: #hors-classement}

Collective innovations involve many more engineers than the twenty in the ranking. When one of them is mentioned in an entry, he may have his own entry, marked **HC** (unranked) in the index. An unranked entry documents a contribution, with the same sourcing standards; it receives no rank, no tier and no score, and does not alter the ranking of the twenty.
