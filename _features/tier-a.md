Tier A brings together **paradigm revolutionaries**. Their records share several traits: a lasting change to the technical grammar of F1, a clear "before / after", diffusion across the whole grid, an identifiable personal contribution within often collective achievements, and an impact that goes beyond a single car or a single set of regulations.

## Five questions transformed

Rather than a simple rank, each member of Tier A can be associated with a fundamental question:

| Engineer | Question transformed |
|---|---|
| **John Cooper** | Where should the car's fundamental components be placed? |
| **Colin Chapman** | How should the car be built and structured? |
| **Keith Duckworth** | How should the power unit be integrated and supplied? |
| **John Barnard** | With which materials and interfaces should the modern F1 car be built? |
| **Adrian Newey** | How can the whole be optimised as an integrated aerodynamic system? |

This is the key to reading the whole ranking.

## No. 1 — Colin Chapman (96/100)

Chapman accumulates several architectural changes rather than a single breakthrough: the aluminium monocoque of the Lotus 25 (layout by Chapman, detail drawing by [Alan Styman]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/alan-styman.md %}) and Ian Jones, riveted assembly worked out by [Mike Costin]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/mike-costin.md %})), the relentless pursuit of low weight, the engine used as a structural member (generalised with the Lotus 49; the Lancia D50 used it as early as 1954), an early exploitation of aerodynamics, and the leadership of the Lotus programme that led to ground effect, carried out with [Peter Wright]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/peter-wright.md %}) and [Tony Rudd]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/tony-rudd.md %}). The 1970 Lotus 72, with its side-mounted radiators, was co-designed with [Maurice Philippe]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/maurice-philippe.md %}), though their respective shares remain disputed. **Several breakthroughs, one philosophy: architecture, lightness, aero.**

See the [Colin Chapman]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/colin-chapman.md %}) entry

## No. 2 — John Barnard (95/100)

Two independent breakthroughs, both of which became universal. The **1981 McLaren MP4/1**, designed by Barnard with a team that included [Steve Nichols]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/steve-nichols.md %}) and [Alan Jenkins]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/alan-jenkins.md %}), was the first F1 car with a carbon composite monocoque to race in a Grand Prix. The Lotus 88, whose carbon monocoque used different construction techniques according to Autosport, ran in practice at Long Beach in March 1981, before the MP4/1's race debut, but was never allowed to race. The **1989 Ferrari 640** then introduced the semi-automatic gearbox controlled from the steering wheel. In this ranking, Barnard is probably the revolutionary whose personal authorship is the clearest.

See the [John Barnard]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/john-barnard.md %}) entry

## No. 3 — John Cooper (93.5/100)

The decisive switch from front engine to **mid-engine** — a layout Cooper did not invent (the pre-war Auto Unions already used it), but which he popularised and made a winner — changed at a stroke the weight distribution, the polar moment of inertia, traction, packaging, the chassis and future aerodynamics. After Cooper's titles in 1959 and 1960, the front engine became obsolete. Credit is shared with [Owen Maddock]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/owen-maddock.md %}), Cooper's chief designer during those two titles.

See the [John Cooper]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/john-cooper.md %}) entry

## No. 4 — Keith Duckworth (92.5/100)

The **Cosworth DFV** transformed the engine and the economics of the grid: compact and powerful, used as a structural component on the Lotus 49, then available to many teams, which could concentrate their resources on the chassis and aerodynamics. It won **more than 150 world championship Grands Prix** from 1967 to 1983, the last of them in its DFY version (the exact count varies between sources: see the entry). The DFV remains a Cosworth achievement, with **[Mike Costin]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/mike-costin.md %})** in the causal chain.

See the [Keith Duckworth]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/keith-duckworth.md %}) entry

## No. 5 — Adrian Newey (91.5/100)

There is no point looking for "the Newey invention". His contribution is a **systemic method**: designing the car as an integrated aerodynamic system, in which suspension, geometry, cooling, structure and packaging all serve overall control of the airflow. He has demonstrated it under several sets of regulations, at March (which became Leyton House), Williams, McLaren and Red Bull, then Aston Martin since 2025. What sets him apart is repetition, not the one-off stroke.

See the [Adrian Newey]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/adrian-newey.md %}) entry

## Why isn't Newey number one?

His record counts for only 10% in the method. His authorship is necessarily more distributed within the large engineering organisations of today, and he is not associated with a one-off breakthrough comparable to carbon or the rear engine. His case rests on a different criterion: **staying at the top of aerodynamic engineering across several generations of regulations.**

## Chapman versus Barnard: the real debate

With this method, the most serious debate for first place is not "Chapman versus Newey" but **Chapman versus Barnard**.

| Criterion | Chapman | Barnard |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| Number of breakthroughs | ++ | + |
| Direct authorship | + | ++ |
| Architectural reach | ++ | ++ |
| Diffusion | ++ | ++ |
| Repetition | ++ | + |

Chapman wins on **historical breadth**; Barnard wins on **personal traceability**. If the weighting for "direct authorship" were raised significantly, Barnard could move ahead.
