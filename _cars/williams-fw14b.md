## What it changed

"The main difference between the FW14 [used in 1991] and the FW14B was the active suspension," says Patrick Head{% include src.html n="1" %}. The [active suspension]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#suspension-active) system kept the car level whatever the loads, and so gave the aerodynamics a stable platform{% include src.html n="3" %}.

The story began in 1985{% include src.html n="1" %}. The brake manufacturer AP was developing active suspension for road cars; it gave up and, rather than bury the project, offered it to Williams{% include src.html n="1" %}. Purely mechanical at first, the system became electronic, programmed to give the driver whatever understeer or oversteer he wanted{% include src.html n="1,3" %}.

The FW14B also combined [traction control]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#antipatinage) and a [semi-automatic gearbox]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#boite-semi-automatique){% include src.html n="2" %}. The gearbox had cost a lot of points in 1991 through unreliability; once sorted, it allowed, according to Head, gear changes four or five times faster and protected the engine from over-revving{% include src.html n="1" %}. The Renault [V10]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#architecture-moteur), powerful and reliable, was in his view the best racing engine of the time{% include src.html n="1,2" %}.

## Who designed it

Williams and Autosport credit [Patrick Head]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/patrick-head.md %}), technical director, and [Adrian Newey]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/adrian-newey.md %}){% include src.html n="1,2" %}. The active suspension was a chain of work: [Frank Dernie]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/frank-dernie.md %}) designed the first system (1987) before leaving for Lotus in 1989 (GrandPrix.com){% include src.html n="7,9" %}; Williams brought it back in enhanced form in 1992{% include src.html n="7" %}, and [Paddy Lowe]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/paddy-lowe.md %}) wrote most of the software for that version (formula1.com){% include src.html n="4" %}. According to Head, Newey played no part in developing the active system{% include src.html n="8" %}.

## Limitations

- The FW14B was only an adaptation{% include src.html n="4" %}. As formula1.com points out, it was an existing car with active suspension added, whereas the FW15C would be designed around it from the outset{% include src.html n="4" %}.
- The system was mainly reactive{% include src.html n="1" %}. On corner entry, the car floated for a brief moment before settling{% include src.html n="1" %}. Mansell accepted that sensation for the grip that followed; Riccardo Patrese, for his part, wanted more feel{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Legacy

- **1992**: ten wins, nine of them for Mansell; the tenth went to Patrese, in Japan{% include src.html n="2,6" %}. Mansell clinched the world title in Hungary, round eleven of sixteen, with five races to spare, and Williams won the constructors' title{% include src.html n="2,3" %}.
- The car was so competitive that Williams dropped plans to race the FW15 intended for 1992{% include src.html n="2" %}.
- In 1994, active suspension was banned, along with other driver aids such as traction control{% include src.html n="5" %}.
