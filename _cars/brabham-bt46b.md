## What it changed

In 1978, the [Lotus 79]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _cars/lotus-79.md %}) took [ground effect]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#effet-de-sol) further. Brabham could not follow: the Alfa Romeo [flat-12]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#flat-12) was too wide to leave room for venturi sidepods. Gordon Murray took another route.

The BT46B carried a large [fan]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#voiture-a-ventilateur) at the rear, driven from the gearbox. A radiator sat on top of the engine. Under the car, a large area was sealed by four interconnected [skirts]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#jupes), and the fan extracted the air from it. The decisive point, according to Motor Sport: [downforce]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#appui) depended on engine revs, not on the car's speed.

The regulations banned movable aerodynamic devices. Brabham argued that the fan's primary purpose was to cool the engine, "primary" meaning, according to the lawyers consulted, more than 50%.

## Who designed it

[Gordon Murray]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/gordon-murray.md %}) led the project, but the origin of the idea is disputed, within a single Motor Sport article (1998):

- Murray himself does not remember exactly where it came from;
- Gary Anderson claims he suggested fitting a fan;
- engineer David Cox claims a share of it: he says he glimpsed a drawing of a fan on Maurice Philippe's drawing board at Tyrrell.

All agree on the source of inspiration: the Chaparral 2J from Can-Am, the 1970 "sucker" car. And on the execution: "The Brabham design team did a wonderful job of putting the idea into practice."

## Limitations

The BT46B raced only once: at the Swedish Grand Prix, at Anderstorp, on 17 June 1978, where Niki Lauda won. Motor Sport gives two versions of what happened next:

- a 2025 article says that Brabham withdrew the car of its own accord;
- the 1998 article relates that a quiet withdrawal after 1 August was first negotiated within the constructors' association, and that the sporting authority then banned it with immediate effect, "on safety grounds".

## Legacy

An innovation withdrawn after one race does not prove lasting influence. Motor Sport drew the lesson in 1998: what mattered most was not the technology, but Bernie Ecclestone's agreement to withdraw it and placate the other teams.
