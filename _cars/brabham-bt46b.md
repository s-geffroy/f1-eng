## What it changed

In 1978, the [Lotus 79]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _cars/lotus-79.md %}) took [ground effect]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#effet-de-sol) further{% include src.html n="1" %}. Brabham could not follow: the Alfa Romeo [flat-12]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#flat-12) was too wide to leave room for venturi [sidepods]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#pontons){% include src.html n="1" %}. Gordon Murray took another route{% include src.html n="1" %}.

The BT46B carried a large [fan]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#voiture-a-ventilateur) at the rear, driven from the gearbox{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. A radiator sat on top of the engine{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. Under the car, a large area was sealed by four interconnected [skirts]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#jupes), and the fan extracted the air from it{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. The decisive point, according to Motor Sport: [downforce]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#appui) depended on engine revs, not on the car's speed{% include src.html n="1" %}.

The regulations banned movable aerodynamic devices{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. Brabham argued that the fan's primary purpose was to cool the engine{% include src.html n="2,3" %}, "primary" meaning, according to the lawyers consulted, more than 50%{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Who designed it

[Gordon Murray]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/gordon-murray.md %}) led the project, but the origin of the idea is disputed, within a single Motor Sport article (1998){% include src.html n="1" %}:

- Murray himself does not remember exactly where it came from{% include src.html n="1" %};
- Gary Anderson claims he suggested fitting a fan{% include src.html n="1" %};
- engineer David Cox claims a share of it: he says he glimpsed a drawing of a fan on [Maurice Philippe]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/maurice-philippe.md %})'s drawing board at Tyrrell{% include src.html n="1" %}.

According to Motor Sport, the common inspiration was the Chaparral 2J from Can-Am, the 1970 "sucker" car, which Murray confirms{% include src.html n="1" %}; Goodwood, however, considers the influence uncertain{% include src.html n="4" %}. Motor Sport also praises the Brabham design team for the way it put the idea into practice{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Limitations

The BT46B raced only once: at the Swedish Grand Prix, at Anderstorp, on 17 June 1978, where Niki Lauda won{% include src.html n="1,2,3" %}. Motor Sport gives two versions of what happened next:

- a 2025 article says that Brabham withdrew the car of its own accord{% include src.html n="2,3" %};
- the 1998 article relates that a quiet withdrawal after 1 August was first negotiated within the constructors' association, and that the sporting authority then banned it with immediate effect, "on safety grounds"{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Legacy

An innovation withdrawn after one race does not prove lasting influence. In 1998, John Watson drew the lesson in Motor Sport: what mattered most was not the technology, but Bernie Ecclestone's agreement to withdraw it and placate the other teams{% include src.html n="1" %}.
