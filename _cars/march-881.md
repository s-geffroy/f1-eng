## What it changed

In 1988, its second season since returning to F1, March had a naturally aspirated Judd engine of about 580 bhp, against far more powerful turbos{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. To stand any chance, Edd Straw writes, the car had to be highly efficient aerodynamically{% include src.html n="2" %}. Adrian Newey sums up the era: with a turbo, the way to go faster was more boost and ever bigger rear wings{% include src.html n="1" %}.

The 881 treated the front differently. Until then, according to formula1.com, the convention was a bulky nose flanked by two separate flaps{% include src.html n="2" %}. Newey merged the nose and front [wing]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#aileron) into a single assembly, the wing spanning the full width of the car for maximum [downforce]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#appui){% include src.html n="2" %}. The assembly ensured clean airflow beneath the wing, and so to the floor{% include src.html n="2" %}; and the underside of the nose was undercut: the low pressure there drew air onto the wing and increased downforce{% include src.html n="2" %}.

formula1.com calls it "effectively the prototype" of the aerodynamically driven F1 car, and the conceptual start of the [raised nose]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#nez-releve) era{% include src.html n="2" %}. According to the same source, it also started the trend for more complex three-dimensional endplates{% include src.html n="2" %}.

**A point of divergence.** Newey told Motor Sport that the 881, not the 1990 Tyrrell, was the first raised-nose F1 car: "we raised the nose underneath and tried to make it an extension of the front wing"{% include src.html n="1" %}. Goodwood also speaks of a raised nose{% include src.html n="3" %}. formula1.com is more nuanced: the 881 opened the raised-nose era in its objectives, "even though the nose of the March itself was flat"{% include src.html n="2" %}. This site keeps the Tyrrell 019 as the first raised-nose car, and the 881 as the step before it.

## Who designed it

[Adrian Newey]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/adrian-newey.md %}), who returned to March after race-engineering Mario Andretti in IndyCar{% include src.html n="1" %}. It was his first F1 design{% include src.html n="3" %}. The sources consulted do not name other members of the design office. The engine was the work of John Judd, who derived it, with Honda's permission, from a Honda design{% include src.html n="1" %}. The team was funded by Akira Akagi, who was then promoting his Leyton House brand{% include src.html n="1,3" %}.

## Limitations

- **A cockpit too tight.** Newey designed the car around the small Ivan Capelli{% include src.html n="1" %}. Capelli set the dimension himself in the mock-up, but felt squashed once he drove it; "our inexperience and his", says Newey{% include src.html n="1" %}. The second driver, Maurício Gugelmin, taller and stockier, fitted even less well{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **Understeer.** The car initially suffered from low-speed mid-corner understeer, addressed with mid-season changes{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Legacy

- **1988**: no wins{% include src.html n="2" %}. Capelli finished second at the Portuguese Grand Prix; in Portugal and then Spain, he overtook Ayrton Senna's McLaren{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. In Japan he briefly led Alain Prost across the line, on lap 16{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. Capelli was seventh in the championship{% include src.html n="3" %}. March, sixth among constructors with 22 points, finished ahead of Williams, the other Judd-powered team (20 points){% include src.html n="3,4" %}.
- **1989**: Gugelmin finished third in Brazil in the old 881, before the CG891 arrived{% include src.html n="1,3" %}.
