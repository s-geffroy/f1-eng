## What it changed

In 1988, its second season since returning to F1, March had a naturally aspirated Judd engine of about 580bhp, against far more powerful turbos. To stand any chance, Edd Straw writes, the car had to be highly efficient aerodynamically. Adrian Newey sums up the era: with a turbo, the way to go faster was more boost and ever bigger rear wings.

The 881 treated the front differently. Until then, according to formula1.com, the convention was a bulky nose flanked by two separate flaps. Newey merged the nose and front [wing]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#aileron) into a single assembly, the wing spanning the full width of the car for maximum [downforce]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#appui). The assembly ensured clean airflow beneath the wing, and so to the floor; and the underside of the nose was undercut: the low pressure there drew air onto the wing and increased downforce.

formula1.com calls it "effectively the prototype" of the aerodynamically driven F1 car, and the conceptual start of the [raised nose]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#nez-releve) era. According to the same source, it also started the trend for more complex three-dimensional endplates.

**A point of divergence.** Newey told Motor Sport that the 881, not the 1990 Tyrrell, was the first raised-nose F1 car: "we raised the nose underneath and tried to make it an extension of the front wing". Goodwood also speaks of a raised nose. formula1.com is more nuanced: the 881 opened the raised-nose era in its objectives, "even though the nose of the March itself was flat". This site keeps the Tyrrell 019 as the first raised-nose car, and the 881 as the step before it.

## Who designed it

[Adrian Newey]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/adrian-newey.md %}), who returned to March after race-engineering Mario Andretti in IndyCar. It was his first F1 design. The sources consulted do not name other members of the design office. The engine was the work of John Judd, who derived it, with Honda's permission, from a Honda design. The team was funded by Akira Akagi, who was then promoting his Leyton House brand.

## Limitations

- **A cockpit too tight.** Newey designed the car around the small Ivan Capelli. Capelli set the dimension himself in the mock-up, but felt squashed once he drove it; "our inexperience and his", says Newey. The second driver, Maurício Gugelmin, taller and stockier, fitted even less well.
- **Understeer.** The car initially suffered from low-speed mid-corner understeer, addressed with mid-season changes.

## Legacy

- **1988**: no wins. Capelli finished second at the Portuguese Grand Prix; in Portugal and then Spain, he overtook Ayrton Senna's McLaren. In Japan he briefly led Alain Prost across the line, on lap 16. Capelli was seventh in the championship. March, sixth among constructors with 22 points, finished ahead of Williams, the other Judd-powered team (20 points).
- **1989**: Gugelmin finished third in Brazil in the old 881, before the CG891 arrived.
