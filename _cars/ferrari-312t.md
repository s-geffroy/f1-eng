## What it changed

The “T” in 312T refers to its “trasversale” gearbox, as Motor Sport writes. The gearbox shafts are aligned across the car rather than along its axis: according to Motor Sport, most of the gearbox's mass thus ends up ahead of the rear axle, within the wheelbase. Formula 1 presents this [transverse gearbox]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#boite-transversale) as a first in F1.

The aim, as Alan Henry recalls: to achieve the lowest possible [polar moment of inertia]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#moment-polaire), by gathering as much mass as possible between the axles. It was the culmination of an approach begun on the 312B3, which already sought to [centralise the masses]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#centralisation-des-masses) to make the car more agile.

## Who designed it

[Mauro Forghieri]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/mauro-forghieri.md %}) designed the car. Its [flat-12]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#flat-12) was created in collaboration with [Franco Rocchi]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/franco-rocchi.md %}), whose engine team Motor Sport praises. The same magazine points out that Forghieri was partly following a path opened in 1972 by [Derek Gardner]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/derek-gardner.md %}) at Tyrrell and [Robin Herd]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/robin-herd.md %}) at March.

## Limitations

Niki Lauda at first found the new car much more demanding to drive than the 312B3. The debut at Kyalami, in March 1975, went wrong: Lauda went off in practice on another car's oil, then his engine lacked power in the race because of a belt that had jumped some teeth. Lauda later changed his mind: “The chassis was just perfect; it was totally neutral and progressive.”

## Legacy

- **1975**: Lauda became world champion, and Ferrari won its first constructors' title since 1964.
- The 312T began a series with transverse gearbox and flat-12 that ran up to the 312T5.
