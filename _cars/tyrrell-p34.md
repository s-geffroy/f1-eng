## What it changed

The Tyrrell P34 is the only six-wheeled car ever to start a grand prix: four small steered wheels at the front, two normal-sized wheels at the rear.

When it was unveiled, people assumed the aim was a smaller frontal area. That was not the point, Motor Sport explains: frontal area was set by the huge rear tyres. According to Motor Sport, the real reason was [downforce]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#appui). An exposed wheel in the airflow generates lift, and the bigger the wheel, the greater the lift. Smaller wheels, twice as many of them, keep the rubber and braking area while generating less lift. Other teams countered the lift of their front tyres with more front wing; the Tyrrell could do without, and gain on the straights. Derek Gardner had put a figure on it: "40-odd horsepower".

The context explains the approach. Almost the whole field had the same engine, the same gearbox and the same tyres. "We needed an unfair advantage," said Gardner, "and the six-wheeler was it."

Goodyear agreed to make the tiny front tyres. Gardner wanted nine-inch; they compromised on ten.

## Who designed it

[Derek Gardner]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/derek-gardner.md %}), with Ken Tyrrell's approval. The idea dated back to 1968 and a quite different purpose. Gardner was then working on the four-wheel drive of the Lotus turbine car at Indianapolis, and later of the Matra F1 car; spreading the load over four front wheels, two of them driven, was meant to steady those cars. He proposed a six-wheeler to Andy Granatelli of STP, to no avail. He revived the project at Tyrrell, with a different objective.

The first prototype used the rear end of the 1975 Tyrrell 007. Gardner left the team during the 1977 season, right after Monza by his own account. [Maurice Philippe]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/maurice-philippe.md %}) then widened the front track and moved the oil radiators forward to recover the lost front-end grip; according to Motor Sport, it did not work. Gardner had hired him a few months earlier to fit the Renault turbo V6 into the six-wheeler, a project that never got beyond a mock-up.

## Limitations

- **The front tyres.** The small Goodyears were used by one team only. Goodyear developed the rears and the normal fronts, and the P34's fronts stood still: "the advantage of the six-wheel concept was going rapidly out of the window," said Gardner. Their cross-ply construction was another problem: stiffening the carcass also stiffened the sidewall, and under braking the tyre collapsed onto the rim. Michelin offered a ten-inch radial, but too late.
- **The brakes.** The brake fluid overheated inside the small wheels. At the first race, Depailler went off for that reason. Braking also had to be balanced across four front wheels.
- **1977.** The enveloping glassfibre bodywork was heavy, until Kevlar arrived later in the year.
- **A sceptical driver.** Jody Scheckter, who won with it, called it "a rubbish car". He said he never agreed with what he saw as its two fundamental concepts, reducing frontal area and improving braking; his reading differs from Gardner's.

## Legacy

- **1976**: debut at the Spanish Grand Prix at Jarama, the fourth round; Patrick Depailler qualified behind only Hunt and Lauda. On 13 June at Anderstorp, Scheckter took the car's only pole and won ahead of Depailler. According to Motor Sport, one or other driver reached the podium ten times in thirteen races. Scheckter finished third in the championship, Depailler fourth, and Tyrrell third among constructors, with 71 points.
- **1977**: one second place and three thirds. Depailler and Ronnie Peterson finished tenth and fourteenth. In testing at Silverstone, Karl Kempf, from Goodyear's racing division, fitted the car with sensors and went through the data with Derek Gardner; in his words, even detailed measurements “couldn't save it”.
- In 1978 the Tyrrell 008 went back to four wheels; a four-wheel maximum rule later banned six-wheelers, in 1982 or 1983 depending on the source.
