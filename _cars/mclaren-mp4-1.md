## What it changed

John Barnard's starting point was aerodynamic{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. In the [ground effect]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#effet-de-sol) era, he wanted the largest possible wings under the car, and therefore a very narrow tub, "not to much bigger than the driver's bum"{% include src.html n="1" %} [sic]. Aluminium could not be both that narrow and stiff enough{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. [Carbon composite]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#composite-carbone) could: Autosport sums up the idea as a strong central tub no wider than the Cosworth DFV engine{% include src.html n="3" %}.

McLaren presents it as the first F1 car designed in carbon composite{% include src.html n="1" %}. Because no one yet knew how to mould curved parts, Hercules, in the United States, assembled the first [monocoque]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#monocoque) from five large flat-faced elements{% include src.html n="1" %}. Only one major part remained in aluminium: the internal bulkhead carrying the front suspension{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Who designed it

[John Barnard]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/john-barnard.md %}) was its designer, supported notably by [Steve Nichols]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/steve-nichols.md %}), who by his account joined Barnard and [Alan Jenkins]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/alan-jenkins.md %}): "just the three of us"{% include src.html n="1,5" %}.

- **The Hercules contact.** Barnard says it was Nichols who pointed him towards Hercules Aerospace{% include src.html n="2,5" %}. McLaren's page instead attributes it to an acquaintance of Barnard's from his IndyCar years, without giving a name{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **The materials calculations.** According to Autosport, British Aerospace introduced Barnard to the aeronautical engineer [Arthur Webb]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/arthur-webb.md %}), who gave him the materials science he needed{% include src.html n="3" %}.

## Limitations

- [Colin Chapman]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/colin-chapman.md %}) publicly argued that an all-carbon car like the one McLaren was preparing would not be safe enough{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- The car was prone to porpoising, and McLaren acknowledges that it was never easy to drive{% include src.html n="1,3" %}.
- "First" needs qualifying: the [Lotus 88]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _cars/lotus-88.md %}), also in carbon, ran in practice at Long Beach in March 1981, before the MP4/1's race debut{% include src.html n="4" %}. But the Lotus 88 was never allowed to race{% include src.html n="4" %}.

## Legacy

- **Silverstone, 1981**: John Watson won the British Grand Prix{% include src.html n="1" %}. It was McLaren's first win in four years, and the first for a carbon car{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **Monza, 1981**: Watson crashed heavily at the Lesmo corners, and the car was split in two{% include src.html n="1" %}. He walked away unhurt{% include src.html n="1" %}. "That smash put myths about the carbon monocoque to bed,"{% include src.html n="2" %} says Barnard. The British Civil Aviation Authority then asked to examine the tub, in order to write its own rules for carbon aircraft{% include src.html n="2" %}.
- According to McLaren, the car started a trend as influential as Chapman's monocoque twenty years earlier{% include src.html n="1" %}.
