## What it changed

John Barnard's starting point was aerodynamic. In the [ground effect]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#effet-de-sol) era, he wanted the largest possible wings under the car, and therefore a very narrow tub, "not to much bigger than the driver's bum" [sic]. Aluminium could not be both that narrow and stiff enough. [Carbon composite]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#composite-carbone) could: Autosport sums up the idea as a strong central tub no wider than the Cosworth DFV engine.

McLaren presents it as the first F1 car designed in carbon composite. Because no one yet knew how to mould curved parts, Hercules, in the United States, assembled the first [monocoque]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#monocoque) from five large flat-faced elements. Only one major part remained in aluminium: the internal bulkhead carrying the front suspension.

## Who designed it

[John Barnard]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/john-barnard.md %}) was its designer, supported notably by [Steve Nichols]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/steve-nichols.md %}), who by his account joined Barnard and [Alan Jenkins]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/alan-jenkins.md %}): "just the three of us".

- **The Hercules contact.** Barnard says it was Nichols who pointed him towards Hercules Aerospace. McLaren's page instead attributes it to an acquaintance of Barnard's from his IndyCar years, without giving a name.
- **The materials calculations.** According to Autosport, British Aerospace introduced Barnard to the aeronautical engineer [Arthur Webb]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/arthur-webb.md %}), who gave him the materials science he needed.

## Limitations

- [Colin Chapman]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/colin-chapman.md %}) publicly argued that an all-carbon car like the one McLaren was preparing would not be safe enough.
- The car was prone to porpoising, and McLaren acknowledges that it was never easy to drive.
- "First" needs qualifying: the [Lotus 88]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _cars/lotus-88.md %}), also in carbon, ran in practice at Long Beach in March 1981, before the MP4/1's race debut. But the Lotus 88 was never allowed to race.

## Legacy

- **Silverstone, 1981**: John Watson won the British Grand Prix. It was McLaren's first win in four years, and the first for a carbon car.
- **Monza, 1981**: Watson crashed heavily at the Lesmo corners, and the car was split in two. He walked away unhurt. "That smash put myths about the carbon monocoque to bed," says Barnard. The British Civil Aviation Authority then asked to examine the tub, in order to write its own rules for carbon aircraft.
- According to McLaren, the car started a trend as influential as Chapman's monocoque twenty years earlier.
