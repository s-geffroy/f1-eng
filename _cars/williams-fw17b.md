## What it changed

The FW17B was not a new car: it was the 1995 FW17 in its end-of-season form{% include src.html n="3" %}. According to Autosport, it received revised suspension and aerodynamics and a new gearbox{% include src.html n="3" %}. Its lasting contribution lay elsewhere, under the back of the car.

The 1995 rules imposed a stepped floor: a raised step plane, 50mm above the reference plane on each side{% include src.html n="1,2,13" %}. [Adrian Newey]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/adrian-newey.md %}) explained it to Motor Sport: unlike the reference plane, the wording did not require the step plane to run all the way to the rear axle{% include src.html n="1" %}. It could therefore stop at the edge of the rear wheel, which left a "grey area": you could cut above the reference plane and enlarge the exit of the [diffuser]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#diffuseur){% include src.html n="1" %}.

The FW17B's diffuser was accordingly undercut and ran up over the exhausts rather than beneath them{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. "The diffusers were very exit-limited," Newey explains: a larger exit draws far more air under the car{% include src.html n="1" %}. According to him, the solution appeared on the FW17B at Estoril, late in 1995{% include src.html n="1" %}. He expected it to be copied for 1996, having shown it at "the worst possible time"; his rivals did not{% include src.html n="1" %}.

- **Engine**: Renault RS7 V10 of 3 litres, the capacity in force from 1995{% include src.html n="11,12" %}.

## Who designed it

GrandPrix.com credits the FW17 to Adrian Newey and [Patrick Head]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/patrick-head.md %}), with their team of design engineers at Didcot{% include src.html n="4" %}; Autosport names only Newey{% include src.html n="3" %}. Newey and formula1.com present the diffuser idea as his, but Newey speaks in the plural: "we actually introduced it on the FW17B"{% include src.html n="1,2" %}.

The FW17 itself continued the line of the FW14, FW15 and FW16, according to GrandPrix.com{% include src.html n="4" %}. It adopted the [raised nose]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#nez-releve) of the 1994 Benetton, which Head felt gave "a slight advantage" under the new rules, and a roomier cockpit for its two tall drivers, Damon Hill and David Coulthard{% include src.html n="4" %}.

## Limitations

- **A qualifying car first.** At Estoril, according to GrandPrix.com, the FW17B was clearly quicker than the FW17 and was used in qualifying; the team went back to the FW17 for the race, for reliability's sake{% include src.html n="5" %}. Coulthard won his first Grand Prix there, from pole position, in a standard FW17{% include src.html n="3,5" %}.
- **An uncertain label.** Reports do not always name the car the same way: for Hill's win in Australia, Motor Sport lists an FW17B, GrandPrix.com an FW17{% include src.html n="6,7" %}.
- **A disputed record.** GrandPrix.com wrote in 1996 that the FW17B "dominated the end of last season"{% include src.html n="8" %}. The official results are more nuanced: after Portugal, Michael Schumacher won three of the last four races for Benetton (Europe, Pacific, Japan){% include src.html n="9" %}; Hill won the last, in Adelaide, by two laps{% include src.html n="6,7" %}.

## Legacy

- **1995**, for the whole FW17 family: five wins (Hill in Argentina, San Marino, Hungary and Australia; Coulthard in Portugal){% include src.html n="9" %} and, according to Autosport, twelve pole positions from seventeen races{% include src.html n="3" %}. Williams were second in the constructors' championship with 112 points, to Benetton's 137{% include src.html n="10" %}.
- **The 1996 FW18** was developed from the FW17B{% include src.html n="8" %}. "It was sensible to take the root of that car and develop it," said Newey; it kept its six-speed transverse [semi-automatic gearbox]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#boite-semi-automatique){% include src.html n="8" %}. According to formula1.com, the undercut diffuser became a key advantage of the 1996 Williams{% include src.html n="2" %}, and Motor Sport speaks of a "massive aerodynamic advantage"{% include src.html n="1" %}.
