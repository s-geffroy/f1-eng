## What it changed

For 1994 the FIA banned driver aids: active suspension and [traction control]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#antipatinage) were gone. According to Motor Sport, teams started from a clean sheet and looked for grip through aerodynamics and mechanical development. In-race [refuelling]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#ravitaillement) returned at the same time, opening up new strategies.

What set the B194 apart was first of all its timing. "We started designing the 1994 car at the beginning of 1993," Ross Brawn told formula1.com, which was then "almost unheard of". In Motor Sport he places the start as soon as the 1993 car was finished, with one aim: "a quantum leap" in performance. The 1993 car had active suspension; the 1994 car returned to passive suspension.

Brawn describes it as "a very, very light car": the team could place ballast very low to lower the centre of gravity, an idea he calls "not original, but very important". The engine was a V8, not a V10. Brawn calls it "a lovely little engine", valued for driveability, size and packaging rather than outright power. Goodwood, by contrast, finds it heavy and underpowered, to the point of holding the car back. According to Autosport, it repeatedly broke crankshafts in winter testing, leaving the team short of reliable mileage.

Benetton also made the most of refuelling: according to Motor Sport its pit strategy gave it an edge over Williams; according to Autosport the team wrote its own strategy software. Finally, the 1994 Benetton had a high nose: for 1995 Williams adopted this [raised nose]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#nez-releve), which [Patrick Head]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/patrick-head.md %}) felt gave "a slight advantage" under the new rules, according to GrandPrix.com.

## Who designed it

A team. Autosport credits the design to [Rory Byrne]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/rory-byrne.md %}), chief designer; Motor Sport describes his partnership with [Ross Brawn]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/ross-brawn.md %}), technical director, whom Tom Walkinshaw had brought from his sportscar team. Starting the programme early was Brawn's hallmark. Goodwood counts the B194 among the first championship-winning cars for Brawn, Byrne and [Pat Symonds]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/pat-symonds.md %}), then Schumacher's race engineer according to Autosport. For the engine, Motor Sport hears from Geoff Goddard, then Cosworth's chief designer: the bore he wanted being too big for the HB engine, "we had to build a new head, a new block and a longer crank: Zetec-R".

The engine's name varies between sources: Autosport calls it the Zetec-R, Motor Sport speaks of a Cosworth V8, Goodwood of a Ford V8. Goddard's account makes it an engine designed at Cosworth, for Ford, which, in his words, "didn't even want to be in F1".

## Limitations

- **Suspicion.** Rivals suspected the B194 of being illegal. In the electronics data from Imola, the FIA found a launch-control function, "Option 13", but could not prove it had been used and took no action, according to Motor Sport and Autosport. Benetton denied using it. At the German Grand Prix, the fire on Jos Verstappen's car during a refuelling stop revealed that a filter had been removed from the fuel hose; the team said it had verbal FIA permission and escaped a penalty.
- **Penalties.** At Silverstone Schumacher committed an infringement on the formation lap; he lost his second place and was banned for two races. At Spa his win was taken away: the plank under the car was too worn. Schumacher lost sixteen points in all through disqualification, according to Autosport.
- **After Imola**, the FIA cut down the diffuser, vented the airbox and imposed the plank. According to Autosport, the team felt these changes made its car average; by the end of the season, the Williams was arguably the better car.

Brawn admits the controversy "clouded a little bit" his view of a car he considers "a fabulous design".

## Legacy

- **1994**: eight wins, all for Michael Schumacher, according to the official results; six of the first seven races. The title was decided in Adelaide: Schumacher and Damon Hill collided and retired, and Schumacher was champion by one point.
- Schumacher's team-mates came and went (Jos Verstappen, JJ Lehto, Johnny Herbert) without really supporting him, Goodwood notes: the constructors' title went to Williams, with 118 points to 103.
- The method endured. Brawn sums it up as starting early, with a dedicated and very integrated programme; in his words, the 2014 Mercedes W05 was the result of exactly the same philosophy.
