## What it changed

Since the arrival of the sequential [semi-automatic gearbox]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#boite-semi-automatique) in the early 1990s, gear changes had been getting quicker in small steps: lighter components, finer-tuned controls. Yet every shift still briefly interrupted the torque. According to Motor Sport, the BAR 007 took the next leap with F1's first "seamless" shift transmission.

Honda's Atsushi Mano summed up the principle: "Theoretically, the mechanism allows the change in gears to take place instantly by letting the next gear engage while still driving the previous gear." Keeping two gears engaged for too long would destroy the gearbox. Honda therefore gave each gear a one-way clutch that only unlocked during a shift, so that engine braking remained available. Shock loads on the gear teeth were still high: finer control of the main clutch reduced them.

The average gain was 0.4 seconds per lap, a massive return for a single upgrade. The car also became easier to drive, especially in the wet: a gear change in a corner all but stopped spinning the wheels.

## Who designed it

In 2005 Honda bought BAR's shares and turned it into a true factory team. The technical director was [Geoff Willis]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/geoff-willis.md %}), heading a three-part organisation: BAR Honda GP in Brackley, England, Honda's European engine-development base, and Honda R&D in Japan.

The seamless-shift gearbox was developed at Honda R&D in Tochigi, Japan. According to Honda's specification sheet, the casing was BAR's, the internals Honda's and Xtrac's. Mano pointed to a choice specific to Honda: a single shift barrel, where other teams would use two, one for the even gears and one for the odd. This choice ruled out any chance of selecting two gears at once, and saved weight.

## Limitations

The gearbox did not prevent a difficult season. According to Takeo Kiuchi, Honda's F1 project leader, the downforce target set for the car was too low compared with the other manufacturers: "We set a benchmark that was 20 percent lower, which couldn't be recovered, and ultimately affected us to the end." Honda also cites a sensitive car that was hard to set up, an unreliable engine and Michelin tyres that did not suit it.

At Imola, the fourth round, Button and Takuma Sato crossed the line third and fifth, but the team was disqualified: a difference of interpretation with the governing body over the fuel tank structure also got it banned from the next two races, in Spain and Monaco. Its first points of the season only came in France, the tenth round.

## Legacy

- **2005**: Button took pole in Canada; after France, he reached the podium twice. BAR Honda finished sixth among constructors, with 38 points, having been second in 2004.
- The other teams soon caught on: by 2008, according to Motor Sport, all of them had a similar system.
- For 2009 Honda was preparing a gearbox without shift forks or dog rings, using an internal shift mechanism, 19% shorter and 12% lighter. Its withdrawal from F1 at the end of 2008 meant it never raced.
