## What it changed

Since the arrival of the sequential [semi-automatic gearbox]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#boite-semi-automatique) in the early 1990s, gear changes had been getting quicker in small steps: lighter components, finer-tuned controls{% include src.html n="1" %}. Yet every shift still briefly interrupted the torque{% include src.html n="1" %}. According to Motor Sport, the BAR 007 took the next leap with F1's first "seamless" shift transmission{% include src.html n="1" %}.

Honda's Atsushi Mano summed up the principle: "Theoretically, the mechanism allows the change in gears to take place instantly by letting the next gear engage while still driving the previous gear."{% include src.html n="1" %} Keeping two gears engaged for too long would destroy the gearbox{% include src.html n="1" %}. Honda therefore gave each gear a one-way clutch that only unlocked during a shift, so that engine braking remained available{% include src.html n="1" %}. Shock loads on the gear teeth were still high: finer control of the main clutch reduced them{% include src.html n="1" %}.

The average gain was 0.4 seconds per lap, a massive return for a single upgrade{% include src.html n="1" %}. The car also became easier to drive, especially in the wet: a gear change in a corner hardly made the wheels spin any more{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Who designed it

In 2005 Honda bought BAR's shares and turned it into a true factory team{% include src.html n="2,3" %}. The technical director was [Geoff Willis]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/geoff-willis.md %}), heading a three-part organisation: BAR Honda GP in Brackley (England); Honda's European engine-development base; and Honda R&D in Japan{% include src.html n="2" %}.

The seamless-shift gearbox was developed at Honda R&D in Tochigi, Japan{% include src.html n="2" %}. According to Honda's specification sheet, the casing was BAR's, the internals Honda's and Xtrac's{% include src.html n="2" %}. Mano pointed to a choice specific to Honda: a single shift barrel, where other teams would use two, one for the even gears and one for the odd{% include src.html n="1" %}. This choice ruled out any chance of selecting two gears at once, and saved weight{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Limitations

The gearbox did not prevent a difficult season{% include src.html n="2,3" %}. According to Takeo Kiuchi, Honda's F1 project leader, the downforce target set for the car was too low compared with the other manufacturers: "We set a benchmark that was 20 percent lower, which couldn't be recovered, and ultimately affected us to the end."{% include src.html n="2" %} Honda also cites a sensitive car that was hard to set up, an unreliable engine and Michelin tyres that did not suit it{% include src.html n="2" %}.

At Imola, the fourth round, Button and Takuma Sato crossed the line third and fifth, but the team was disqualified{% include src.html n="2,5" %}: a difference of interpretation with the governing body over the fuel tank structure also got it banned from the next two races, in Spain and Monaco{% include src.html n="2" %}. Its first points of the season only came in France, the tenth round{% include src.html n="2" %}.

## Legacy

- **2005**: Button took pole in Canada; after France, he reached the podium twice{% include src.html n="2" %}. BAR Honda finished sixth among constructors, with 38 points, having been second in 2004{% include src.html n="2,4" %}.
- The other teams soon caught on: by 2008, according to Motor Sport, all of them had a similar system{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- For 2009 Honda was preparing a gearbox without shift forks or dog rings, using an internal shift mechanism, 19% shorter and 12% lighter{% include src.html n="1" %}. Its withdrawal from F1 at the end of 2008 meant it never raced{% include src.html n="1" %}.
