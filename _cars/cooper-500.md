## What it changed

This is not an F1 car but a small 500cc single-seater built in 1946{% include src.html n="1" %}. Its engine, a motorcycle single, sits in the middle of the car, "for largely practical reasons", as the 500 Owners Association puts it{% include src.html n="1" %}. This is the layout Cooper would carry all the way to F1, as the [mid-engine layout]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#moteur-central-arriere){% include src.html n="2,3" %}.

The same source points out that the layout was not new{% include src.html n="1" %}. What made the car good was the combination with light weight, a low [polar moment of inertia]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#moment-polaire) and all-independent suspension: its handling was excellent{% include src.html n="1" %}.

[John Cooper]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/john-cooper.md %}) himself declined to take credit{% include src.html n="3" %}. He told Nigel Roebuck that with a motorcycle engine and chain drive, putting the engine in the back of a 500 was "the only logical thing to do"{% include src.html n="3" %}. A 500, he said, was "a four-wheeled motorcycle — or a baby Auto Union, if you like"{% include src.html n="3" %}.

## Who designed it

John Cooper and his childhood friend Eric Brandon built two cars, one each{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. Charles Cooper, John's father and a trained mechanic, ran a garage in Surbiton{% include src.html n="2" %}; he backed the project and, according to the 500 Owners Association, obtained the engine through his motorcycle contacts{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. The aluminium body was made by a specialist, Charlie Robinson{% include src.html n="1" %}. Brandon's car, slightly longer, had a few revisions, including the dummy radiator grille later seen on production Coopers{% include src.html n="1" %}.

Work on the first car began in June 1946 and took about five weeks{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## How it was built

- **Engine and drivetrain**: an air-cooled 500cc JAP single from speedway racing{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. A chain ran to a Triumph Speed Twin gearbox, and a second chain to the rear axle{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **Chassis**: a ladder frame, with hoops to support the body and stiffen the structure{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **Suspension**: independent at both ends, using Fiat 500 Topolino parts, with lower wishbones and a transverse leaf spring; the wheels and steering also came from the Fiat{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. The two sources describe the borrowing slightly differently: the 500 Owners Association speaks of a crashed Topolino whose front arrangement was reused at the rear, Doug Nye of two front ends from scrapped Topolinos welded back to back{% include src.html n="1,2" %}.

## Limitations

The engine and gearbox mounts could not cope with the torque and vibration of the single{% include src.html n="1" %}. John Cooper, who also over-revved the engine, bent a valve{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Legacy

- **July 1946**: debut at the Prescott hill climb{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **1947**: in May, at the White Hart meeting, John Cooper finished second and Brandon third; in June, Brandon won at Prescott; in July, he won the first post-war race at Gransden Lodge{% include src.html n="1" %}. The first prototype was sold at the end of the year{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- The Cooper Car Company came afterwards, in 1948 according to Doug Nye{% include src.html n="2" %}. The prototype's layout led to the Cooper F1 cars that won in 1958 and took the titles in 1959 and 1960{% include src.html n="2" %}.
