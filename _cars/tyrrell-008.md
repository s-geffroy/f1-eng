## What it changed

After the six-wheeled P34, the Tyrrell 008 went back to a conventional four-wheeled car{% include src.html n="1" %}. Its contribution lay less in its shape than in what it carried: according to Damien Smith (Autosport), "perhaps 008's most significant contribution" was its early [data logging]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#acquisition-de-donnees) electronics{% include src.html n="1" %}.

The system came from Karl Kempf{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. Recruited by Goodyear's racing division to fit sensors to the cars on its tyres, he spent most of his time at Ferrari and at Tyrrell, which was then working on the six-wheeler; then, when Ferrari switched to Michelin, he turned down an offer from [Harvey Postlethwaite]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/harvey-postlethwaite.md %}) at Wolf to work directly for Tyrrell (Mark Glendenning, Racer){% include src.html n="2" %}. The sensors measured suspension deflection, spring and damper loads, steering, throttle and brake position, engine revs, lateral and fore-aft g-forces and yaw rate{% include src.html n="1,2,3" %}. At Tyrrell the recorder sat in the [sidepods]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#pontons); the data went onto an ordinary cassette, which could hold a whole race{% include src.html n="2,3" %}.

Kempf does not claim to have been the first to measure: "we're the first to actually use the measurements on a regular basis"{% include src.html n="2" %}. That "we" means Tyrrell and Ferrari, the two teams that, by his account, really took up the system, with [Derek Gardner]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/derek-gardner.md %}) and [Mauro Forghieri]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/mauro-forghieri.md %}){% include src.html n="2" %}.

**Sources do not describe the system the same way.** Autosport writes that sponsor Data General "introduced a system developed by Dr Karl Kempf using microprocessors"{% include src.html n="1" %}. Jalopnik, citing a 1978 New Scientist article, writes instead that Kempf "plans to put a microprocessor in the car" once he has solved the problems of electrical noise and vibration{% include src.html n="3" %}. Kempf, in Racer, describes a progression: a recorder on the car, then a computer at the track, then a computer on board{% include src.html n="2" %}. His background differs too: "a mathematician by training", with a PhD, according to Racer{% include src.html n="2" %}; an "American physicist", according to Jalopnik{% include src.html n="3" %}.

## How it was built

- **Chassis and engine**: according to Autosport, an aluminium monocoque, the 2,993cc Cosworth DFV [V8]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#architecture-moteur) (around 485bhp), a Hewland FGA 400 five-speed gearbox, Goodyear tyres and a weight of 585kg{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **A hidden fan.** Its most radical feature was to be a [fan]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#voiture-a-ventilateur), mounted behind the fuel cell and ahead of the engine, driven from the end of the crankshaft{% include src.html n="1" %}. It drew air through radiators mounted in the floor, then exhausted it over the inlet trumpets{% include src.html n="1" %}. Tested at Paul Ricard, its only track outing, it gave trouble every time the DFV approached 10,500rpm; Gentry put this down to overheating{% include src.html n="1" %}. The radiators were then moved on top of the bodywork, near the rear wheels{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **Controlled camber.** Philippe had conceived active camber control, first prototyped on his own Ford Cortina{% include src.html n="1" %}. According to Kempf, who designed its electronics and did the calculations, Brian Lisles redesigned the suspension; two knobs, one for the front and one for the rear, could tune the car's handling{% include src.html n="2" %}. That work, he says, "helped us discover some aerodynamic difficulties with 008 that went into 009"{% include src.html n="2" %}.

## Who designed it

[Maurice Philippe]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/maurice-philippe.md %}), who joined Tyrrell in 1977{% include src.html n="1" %}. According to Autosport, the 008 was the first Tyrrell not designed by Derek Gardner, who left around the Italian Grand Prix{% include src.html n="1" %}. Philippe brought in [John Gentry]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/john-gentry.md %}), a draughtsman and engineer who had worked at March, Shadow and Fittipaldi; according to Gentry, Philippe "needed help on the detail stuff"{% include src.html n="1" %}. The two men first worked in a double garage while Gardner was still there{% include src.html n="1" %}. The data logging was Kempf's work, backed first by Goodyear, then by Data General{% include src.html n="1,2,3" %}.

Autosport spells the name "Phillippe"{% include src.html n="1" %}, Racer "Philippe"{% include src.html n="2" %}: the site uses the latter, as in his entry.

## Limitations

- **The fan dropped.** It never went beyond the Paul Ricard test{% include src.html n="1" %}. The Brabham BT46B won in Sweden with its own fan, to Ken Tyrrell's great anger according to Autosport{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- **Active suspension left on the shelf.** According to Racer, the camber control never made it from the test track to a Grand Prix: the team shelved it when ground effect arrived{% include src.html n="2" %}. Richard Jenkins, quoted by Autosport, calls it "effectively F1's first active suspension system"{% include src.html n="1" %}, and Kempf believes he built "the first active suspension car"{% include src.html n="2" %}. The site's glossary credits the 1983 Lotus 92, the first to race.
- **Only one car at the front.** Didier Pironi scored points in four of his first six races, then only once more, at Hockenheim{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Legacy

- **1978**: 16 Grands Prix, one win and four further podiums, no pole position{% include src.html n="1" %}. Patrick Depailler won at Monaco, at his 69th attempt, 22 seconds ahead of Niki Lauda, in chassis 008/3; he was also second in South Africa and Austria{% include src.html n="1" %}. He finished fifth in the championship with 34 points; Pironi was fifteenth with 7{% include src.html n="5" %}. Tyrrell were fourth among constructors, with 38 points{% include src.html n="4" %}.
- It was Tyrrell's last win until the end of 1982{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- The 009 succeeded it; according to Kempf, it inherited the aerodynamic difficulties found on the 008{% include src.html n="2" %}.
