## What it changed

The D50 made its debut at the Spanish Grand Prix, at Pedralbes, in October 1954{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. Its [V8]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#architecture-moteur) was not merely mounted in the chassis: it was a [structural element]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#moteur-porteur) of it{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. The base remained a short [tubular spaceframe]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#chassis-tubulaire), but the engine at the front and the transaxle at the rear formed its two load-bearing ends{% include src.html n="1" %}.

Two other choices served the same aim, a compact and well-balanced car:

- the engine was angled at eight degrees, so that the propeller shaft ran beside the driver, who sat lower{% include src.html n="2" %};
- the fuel was carried in two long side tanks, between the front and rear wheels: the handling changed little between full and empty tanks{% include src.html n="1,2" %}.

These concentrated masses gave it a low [polar moment of inertia]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#moment-polaire){% include src.html n="1" %}. According to Motor Sport, it was the most agile F1 car of its time{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Who designed it

[Vittorio Jano]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/vittorio-jano.md %}) led the team, and Motor Sport credits him with the overall principles{% include src.html n="1" %}. [Ettore Zaccone Mina]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/ettore-zaccone-mina.md %}), in charge of the engine, disputes this attribution: Jano led the team but was not "the designer"; in his view, no one person was solely responsible for the overall design, the major choices came out of collective discussions, and it was [Gianni Lancia]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/gianni-lancia.md %}) who made the final decisions{% include src.html n="2" %}. Chris Nixon adds that several innovations came from Gianni Lancia himself{% include src.html n="2" %}. This site therefore describes it as a team achievement, led by Jano.

## Limitations

- The early days were laborious: the chassis had to be stiffened against torsion, and the oil circulation was at first so poor that the V8 lasted only four or five laps{% include src.html n="2" %}.
- Agile as it was, the car gave little warning: drivers could barely feel the loss of grip coming{% include src.html n="1" %}.
- Power rose as development went on, from about 250hp to some 275hp according to Doug Nye (Motor Sport){% include src.html n="1" %}; Chris Nixon gives 260hp at 8000rpm{% include src.html n="2" %}.

## Legacy

In 1955 the cars passed to Ferrari{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. Renamed Lancia-Ferraris, they gave Juan Manuel Fangio the 1956 world title{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. The stressed-engine principle would become widespread from 1967 onwards, in a rear-engined layout, with the [Lotus 49]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _cars/lotus-49.md %}) and the DFV.
