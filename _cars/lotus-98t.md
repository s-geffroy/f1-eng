## What it changed

The Lotus 98T is the car with which [pneumatic valve return]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#rappel-pneumatique) reached racing{% include src.html n="2,3" %}. On Renault's EF15 B [turbo]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#turbocompresseur) [V6]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#architecture-moteur), the steel springs that close the valves were replaced by compressed gas{% include src.html n="4" %}. According to Renault, the engine brought two major innovations from the start of the 1986 season: static ignition and, "above all", pneumatic valve return, "a pioneering revs-boost solution" that would appear on all F1 engines in the 1990s{% include src.html n="3" %}. Motor Sport adds that each spark plug had its own miniature ignition coil{% include src.html n="4" %}.

The gain was measured in revs. According to Nigel Roebuck (Autosport), the development work gave the drivers "another thousand revs or so"{% include src.html n="1" %}. Jean Sage of Renault Sport reported what the drivers said: they no longer had to change up in awkward places, "like in the middle of a corner"{% include src.html n="1" %}. Motor Sport quotes 12,500rpm and a reliable 900bhp or so in race trim, and well over 1,000bhp in qualifying; the magazine adds that these figures are speculative{% include src.html n="4" %}.

**Sources do not date the debut the same way.** Renault has the engine in service from the start of the season{% include src.html n="3" %}. At the Brazilian Grand Prix, the opening round, Autosport writes that Senna was "using the only valve-springless engine in the race", with "no real power advantage over the regular engines"{% include src.html n="2" %}. But Jean Sage qualified the idea of a Rio debut: "Actually, Patrick Tambay ran one at Monza, but he never knew it."{% include src.html n="1" %} According to him, work on the engine had started in 1984, in great secrecy{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## How it was built

- **Chassis**: according to Motor Sport, a one-piece moulding of [carbon fibre]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#composite-carbone) and aluminium, with bulkheads machined from solid aluminium{% include src.html n="4" %}. Only four chassis were built for the 1986 season{% include src.html n="4" %}.
- **Engine**: Renault's 1.5-litre turbo V6, in EF15 B form, then EF15 C from mid-1986 according to Renault, with two Garrett turbochargers{% include src.html n="3" %}.
- **A scarce engine.** At the Spanish Grand Prix, the second round, Renault had qualifying engines of this type for all its customers, but race engines were still in short supply{% include src.html n="1" %}. "We have only three here," said Jean Sage. "One for Senna, one for Senna's spare car, and one for Ligier."{% include src.html n="1" %} According to Roebuck, Martin Brundle's Tyrrell was not yet running the latest "pneumatic" engine{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Who designed it

According to Motor Sport, the car was designed by [Gérard Ducarouge]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/gerard-ducarouge.md %}) and [Martin Ogilvie]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/martin-ogilvie.md %}){% include src.html n="4" %}. The source does not say how the work was shared; Ducarouge's entry notes that Motor Sport sees him as the technical leader rather than the draughtsman of the cars credited to him{% include src.html n="4" %}.

The engine was Renault Sport's work{% include src.html n="4" %}. Motor Sport credits the EF15 B, an evolution of the 1985 unit, to chief engineer [Bernard Dudot]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/bernard-dudot.md %}){% include src.html n="4" %}; Roebuck also refers to Dudot's "development work"{% include src.html n="1" %}. The idea of pneumatic valve return was older: in July 1995, GrandPrix.com wrote that Renault had pioneered it "back in the early 1980s", "the idea having been dreamed up by [Jean-Pierre Boudy]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/jean-pierre-boudy.md %})", who was replaced at the head of research and development in 1984{% include src.html n="5" %}. The idea, its development and its arrival in racing therefore belong to different people, within the same team.

## Limitations

- **No extra power at first.** In Brazil, according to Autosport, the springless engine brought no real power advantage; the Renault cars lacked power, or rather fuel, against the Hondas and TAGs, and Senna was slower than the leaders on the straight{% include src.html n="2" %}.
- **The engine shortage.** In April, Renault still had only three race engines of this type at the track, shared between Lotus and Ligier{% include src.html n="1,6" %}.
- **The championship.** Despite eight pole positions according to Renault, Senna won only two races and finished fourth in the championship, behind the Williams-Hondas of Mansell and Piquet and Prost's McLaren{% include src.html n="3,6,8" %}.

## Legacy

- **1986**: in Brazil, Senna started from pole and finished second, 34.8 seconds behind Nelson Piquet (Williams-Honda), according to Autosport{% include src.html n="2" %}. He then won the Spanish Grand Prix at Jerez, by 14 thousandths of a second from Nigel Mansell, and the United States Grand Prix in Detroit{% include src.html n="1,6" %}. He was fourth in the championship with 55 points{% include src.html n="8" %}; Lotus were third among constructors, with 58 points{% include src.html n="7" %}. According to Renault, the EF15 B/C engine scored two wins, eight pole positions and eight podiums{% include src.html n="3" %}.
- **Chassis 98T-3**, winner in Spain and Detroit, scored two wins, five pole positions and three further podiums in eight races, according to Motor Sport{% include src.html n="4" %}.
- **The end of an era.** Renault then suspended its F1 involvement; for Renault, the EF15 closed the turbo era{% include src.html n="3" %}. Pneumatic valve return lived on: according to Renault, it was fitted to every F1 engine from the 1990s{% include src.html n="3" %}.
