## What it changed

In 2022 the rules brought back [ground-effect]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#effet-de-sol) cars: [downforce]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#appui) now came mainly from two [venturi]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#venturi) tunnels under the floor, either side of a central keel{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. The RB18 was Red Bull's answer, and its advantage was above all invisible{% include src.html n="3" %}.

According to formula1.com, the key to these rules was not peak downforce in fast corners, with the car pressed to the ground, but how much was retained in slow corners, with the car riding higher{% include src.html n="1" %}. Other teams shaped the central keel like a teardrop{% include src.html n="1,3" %}. The RB18's was irregular, and the roof of its tunnels was higher and more arched: the airflow kept a more consistent volume along their whole length{% include src.html n="1" %}. Peak downforce was in theory lower; GPS data suggested the Ferrari and the Mercedes had more of it in fast corners{% include src.html n="1" %}. But the floor was far less prone to stalling and tolerant of ride-height changes{% include src.html n="1" %}. The RB18 was never seriously troubled by porpoising, the bouncing seen on other cars{% include src.html n="1" %}.

The suspension pointed the same way. Red Bull, like McLaren, swapped its layout around: pull-rods at the front, push-rods at the rear{% include src.html n="1,4" %}. At the front, the geometry was anti-dive: it limited dive under braking, and so the rise of the rear and the loss of downforce that goes with it{% include src.html n="1" %}. At the rear, long travel let the car sink at high speed, cutting drag and giving it a consistent straight-line advantage{% include src.html n="1" %}. That required relatively soft springs, which on other cars would have induced bouncing{% include src.html n="1" %}.

For Honda, the car's main feature lay elsewhere: [sidepods]({{ site.baseurl }}/en/glossary/#pontons) with a deep undercut, their upper surface sloping down towards the rear{% include src.html n="4" %}. Again according to Honda, this solution later became the standard for almost every team{% include src.html n="4" %}. In early 2023, several teams took up the Red Bull's sidepods{% include src.html n="6" %}; Mercedes and Ferrari at first declined to, before adopting sidepods of this type in their turn during the 2023 season{% include src.html n="6,7,8" %}.

## Who designed it

A technical team, but the sources do not agree on the share of [Adrian Newey]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _engineers/adrian-newey.md %}), then chief technical officer{% include src.html n="1" %}:

- according to formula1.com in November 2022, Newey fed into a group including technical director Pierre Waché, head of aerodynamics Enrico Balbo, Rob Marshall, Craig Skinner, Ben Waterhouse and Paul Monaghan; his direct design input was limited to the suspension, a crucial part of the whole{% include src.html n="1" %};
- according to formula1.com in 2024, he also fed key ideas into the concept, drawing on his experience of ground-effect cars, and recognised that downforce had to be delivered dependably{% include src.html n="2" %};
- Honda speaks of development "spearheaded by" Newey{% include src.html n="4" %}, and Motor Sport of an "Adrian Newey-led" team{% include src.html n="3" %}.

Both formula1.com articles agree on one point: Newey personally designed the front and rear suspension{% include src.html n="1,2" %}. He said he was at first dispirited by rules that seemed to leave no room for creativity, before finding "a reasonable degree of freedom" around the sidepods and floor{% include src.html n="3" %}.

## Limitations

At the start of the season the RB18 was well over the minimum weight, mostly at the front: it understeered more than Max Verstappen liked and was prone to locking its front brakes{% include src.html n="1" %}. The weight came down race by race, with a new floor at Imola, redesigned brakes and hollowed-out parts first made solid for lack of time{% include src.html n="1" %}.

## Legacy

- **2022**: seventeen wins from twenty-two races, fifteen for Verstappen and two for Sergio Pérez, according to formula1.com{% include src.html n="3,5" %}; Honda gives a total of nineteen, a figure neither formula1.com nor Motor Sport uses{% include src.html n="3,4,5" %}. Verstappen was world champion{% include src.html n="1,4" %}; Red Bull won the constructors' title for the first time since 2013, with 759 points to Ferrari's 554{% include src.html n="4,9" %}.
- The engine was a Honda: the manufacturer, which had officially left F1 at the end of 2021, developed and built it, but under the name RBPTH001, without the Honda badge{% include src.html n="4" %}. It was an evolution of the 2021 RA621H{% include src.html n="4" %}; formula1.com in fact refers to it as the RA621{% include src.html n="1" %}.
